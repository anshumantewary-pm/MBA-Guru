1. Scrum mechanics, and how they translate to other Agile frameworks such as SAFe, Disciplined Agile and LeSS.
2. Agile principles and how to apply them in any industry, with case studies in software, aerospace, finance and construction.
3. How to reduce the risk of project failure with results-based Agile controls that close projects more effectively.
4. How to increase speed with Lean and Agile work-management techniques proven to deliver faster.
5. How to improve project benefits with innovation and leadership approaches that unlock a team's potential.

The program covers "pure" Agile Scrum and Lean Kanban, and the trade-offs of hybrids: Lean Startup, SAFe and Disciplined Agile. It is aimed at engineers, managers, designers, writers and "executers of all types".

The course is built as **Why, Who, How, What**. It doesn't make you a PMI-ACP or Certified ScrumMaster; it is a foundation in Agile principles and how Scrum and scaled Agile are applied in industry.

### Week 1: Why Agile?

Why Agile methods are used, with case studies that prove they work and the history that led to Scrum.

| Lesson (video) | Length |
|---|---|
| 1.1 Agile Basics | 12 min |
| 1.2 Proof Agile Works | 7 min |
| 1.3 Evolution of Agile | 11 min |
| 1.4 Netflix Case Study | 3 min |
| 1.5 18F Case Study | 2 min |

**Key concepts**
- **Agile Manifesto (2001).** Four values: individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan. Twelve principles sit behind it (early and continuous delivery, welcome changing requirements, frequent delivery, daily business–developer collaboration, motivated individuals, face-to-face conversation, working product as the measure of progress, sustainable pace, technical excellence, simplicity, self-organising teams, regular reflection).
- **Why Agile beats waterfall.** Waterfall fixes scope up front and learns late, so risk piles up at the end. Agile delivers in short increments, so feedback, value and risk reduction come early. Industry studies (for example the Standish CHAOS reports) consistently show Agile projects succeeding more often than waterfall ones.
- **Evolution.** Lean manufacturing (Toyota) → iterative and incremental development → Scrum (Schwaber and Sutherland, 1995) and Extreme Programming → the Agile Manifesto → scaled frameworks.
- **Netflix.** Continuous delivery, small autonomous teams and a "freedom and responsibility" culture, so the company can ship and learn constantly.
- **18F.** The US government digital services team that brought Agile, user-centred delivery to federal projects. It shows that Agile works outside start-ups.

### Week 2: Who uses Agile?

Who uses Agile across industries, the types of work management, and where Scrum fits.

| Lesson (video) | Length |
|---|---|
| Week 2 Introduction | 1 min |
| 2.1 Simple PM Methods | 6 min |
| 2.2 Approaching the Triple Cost Constraint | 8 min |
| 2.3 Comparing Methods Across Industries | 5 min |
| 2.4 Comparing Methods of Customer Management | 6 min |
| 2.5 Comparing Methods of Engineering Management | 10 min |

**Key concepts**
- **Three families of method:** *Traditional* (predictive, plan-driven, waterfall), *Agile* (iterative, value-driven, Scrum) and *Lean* (flow-driven, waste removal, Kanban). The course compares all three.
- **Triple constraint: scope, time, cost** (with quality in the middle). Traditional PM fixes **scope** and estimates time and cost. Agile **inverts the triangle**: it fixes **time** (the sprint) and **cost** (the team), and lets **scope** flex by priority. This is the single most tested idea.
- **Industries.** Software and IT have almost universal Agile adoption. Aerospace, finance, construction and government increasingly use Agile or hybrid methods. Work that is highly regulated or physically sequenced (heavy construction) stays more predictive.
- **Customer management.** Traditional methods use contracts and change control. Agile uses a Product Owner, frequent demos and collaboration.
- **Engineering management.** Big design up front versus emergent design, continuous integration and test-driven work.

### Week 3: How to Scrum and be Agile?

The mechanics of Scrum: team formation, planning and retro "games", and managing work to meet Sprint goals.

| Lesson (video) | Length |
|---|---|
| Introduction to Week 3 | 1 min |
| 3.1 Scrum Team Formation | 11 min |
| 3.2 Three-Part User Story | 8 min |
| 3.3 Sprint Planning | 9 min |
| 3.4 Sprint Development | 8 min |
| 3.5 Sprint Retro and Review | 7 min |

**Key concepts: Scrum in one page (the 3-5-3)**

| Three roles (accountabilities) | Five events | Three artifacts (and their commitments) |
|---|---|---|
| **Product Owner:** owns and orders the Product Backlog, maximises value, the one voice of the customer | **The Sprint:** a fixed time-box of up to one month (often 2 weeks) that holds all the other events | **Product Backlog** → *Product Goal* |
| **Scrum Master:** servant-leader who coaches Scrum, removes impediments and protects the team | **Sprint Planning:** *why* (Sprint Goal), *what* (selected items) and *how* (plan); up to 8 h for a 1-month sprint | **Sprint Backlog** → *Sprint Goal* |
| **Developers:** the cross-functional, self-managing people who build the Increment (team usually 10 people or fewer) | **Daily Scrum:** 15 minutes, progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapting the plan | **Increment** → *Definition of Done* |
| | **Sprint Review:** inspect the Increment with stakeholders and adapt the backlog | |
| | **Sprint Retrospective:** inspect how the team worked and pick improvements | |

- **Three pillars of empiricism:** transparency, inspection, adaptation. **Five values:** commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage.
- **Three-part user story:** *As a* `<user>`, *I want* `<capability>`, *so that* `<benefit>`. Add **acceptance criteria** (often *Given / When / Then*). Good stories are **INVEST**: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
- **Estimating and planning:** story points (relative size, often a Fibonacci scale), Planning Poker, **velocity** (points finished per sprint) to forecast, and backlog refinement to keep the top of the backlog ready.
- **Sprint development:** a task board or Kanban board (To do / Doing / Done), limited work in progress, burn-down charts, the Definition of Done, and no scope changes that put the Sprint Goal at risk.
- **Retro games:** Start / Stop / Continue, Mad / Sad / Glad, the Sailboat (wind and anchors). The output is one or two concrete improvements for the next sprint.
- **Review versus Retro:** the Review is about the **product** (what we built, with stakeholders). The Retro is about the **process** (how we worked, team only). This is a common exam trap.

### Week 4: What Scrum framework fits best?

Scrum at scale, how it differs from other Agile frameworks, program and portfolio management, risk management, and the pitfalls of Agile.

| Lesson (video) | Length |
|---|---|
| Introduction to Week 4 | 1 min |
| 4.1 Scrum in the World of Agile | 11 min |
| 4.2 Exploring the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) | 7 min |
| 4.3 Exploring Disciplined Agile Delivery (DAD) | 9 min |
| 4.4 Exploring the Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) Framework | 7 min |
| 4.5 Pitfalls and Benefits of Agile at Scale | 14 min |

**Key concepts: comparing scaling frameworks**

| Framework | Core idea | Good fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scrum** | One team, one Product Owner, one backlog | A single product team of up to about 10 people | Doesn't say how many teams coordinate |
| **Scrum of Scrums** | Team representatives meet to coordinate | A few teams on one product | Coordination only, no portfolio view |
| **SAFe** | Team, program (Agile Release Train), large-solution and portfolio levels; Program Increment (PI) Planning every 8 to 12 weeks; roles such as Release Train Engineer | Large enterprises needing alignment, governance and a portfolio link | Heavy and prescriptive, can become "waterfall in Agile clothing" |
| **DAD (Disciplined Agile Delivery)** | A process-decision toolkit: choose your way of working (Scrum-based, Kanban/Lean, continuous delivery); full delivery lifecycle of Inception, Construction and Transition | Organisations that need context-specific hybrids (now part of PMI) | Many choices, needs experienced coaches |
| **LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum)** | "More with less": one Product Owner and one Product Backlog across 2 to 8 teams (LeSS Huge for more); minimal extra roles | Organisations willing to restructure around the product | Demands deep organisational change |
| **Kanban / Lean** | Visualise flow, limit WIP, manage cycle time, continuous pull, no fixed sprints | Operations, support and unpredictable flow work | Less rhythm for planning and stakeholder demos |

- **Program and portfolio:** a *project* delivers outputs, a *program* coordinates related projects for benefits, and a *portfolio* chooses the right investments. At scale, Agile links the portfolio backlog (epics) to team backlogs (stories).
- **Risk management in Agile:** short iterations, early demos and a working increment each sprint remove risk continuously instead of at the end. The backlog can hold risk-reduction spikes.
- **Common pitfalls:** "Agile in name only" (stand-ups without empowerment); no real Product Owner; no Definition of Done; management by velocity targets; scaling before single teams work; rigid framework adoption; technical debt; and treating Agile as a tool change rather than a culture change.

### Final exam

45 minutes and cumulative. Many questions ask you to *apply* concepts to scenarios rather than recall them, so expect "what should the Scrum Master or Product Owner do next?" style questions.

## 4. The important things to know (cheat sheet)

1. **Agile fixes time and cost and flexes scope.** Traditional PM fixes scope and flexes time and cost.
2. **Scrum is 3-5-3:** three roles (PO, SM, Developers), five events (Sprint, Planning, Daily, Review, Retro) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment).
3. **The Product Owner owns *what* and *in what order*. The Developers own *how*. The Scrum Master owns *the process*** and removes impediments. They are a servant-leader, not a project manager.
4. **Each artifact has a commitment:** Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done.
5. **Review = product with stakeholders. Retro = process, team only.**
6. **User story = As a / I want / so that, plus acceptance criteria.** Test it against INVEST.
7. **Velocity is for forecasting, not for judging performance.**
8. **Empiricism:** transparency, inspection, adaptation. **Values:** commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage.
9. **Scaling:** SAFe (enterprise, PI Planning, release trains), DAD (choose your way of working, now part of PMI), LeSS (one PO and one backlog for many teams), Kanban (flow, WIP limits).
10. **Agile reduces risk** by delivering working increments early. Most failures are cultural, not technical.
11. **Lean / Kaizen:** remove waste and improve continuously. The program's four promised benefits are Speed, Innovation, Leadership and Kaizen.
12. **PDUs:** 10 per course and 50 for the program. Claim them in PMI's CCR system after you get the certificate.
